Tips
  • Pair iPhone → Car via Bluetooth first. Then open this page and tap Start noise.
  • Keep level modest (−35 to −25 dB). You should barely notice the masker; the cabin should feel smoother, not louder.
  • If conversation feels harder, keep Voice-aware ducking enabled so the masker drops while you speak.
  • Add this page to your Home Screen (Share → Add to Home Screen) to make it feel more like a native app.
What am I seeing? (and what the code is doing)
  • Pink noise: Energy decreases slowly at higher frequencies (more energy in bass/mids, less in treble). This sounds smooth and natural and is a great all-around masker for most cars.
  • Brown noise: Much deeper and heavier. It has a lot of low-frequency energy (rumble region). Best for masking tire hum, road rumble, and wind buffeting, especially in louder cabins.
  • Gray noise: Shaped so all frequencies are perceived as similar loudness to the human ear. It feels “even” and airy. Good for quieter cars or EVs where the noise is more high-frequency than bassy.
  • Cabin (RMS): A rolling average of how strong the sound is at the iPhone mic, measured in dBFS (decibels relative to full scale). 0 dBFS would be a clipped signal, so normal car levels will be negative (for example −35 to −10 dBFS). When you switch RoadCalm ON, this number may go up a bit because we’re adding low-level masking noise.
  • When RoadCalm is “working”: With your car at a steady speed, toggle Start noise ON/OFF. With noise ON, the cabin should sound smoother and less harsh / fatiguing, even if the RMS number goes up. With noise OFF, the road noise usually feels more “raw” and spiky.
  • Spectrum plot: Each bar shows how much energy there is at different frequencies: low rumble on the left, speech and mid-range in the center, hiss/whine on the right. With noise OFF, you often see sharp peaks and dips. With noise ON, the plot should look more filled-in and even, especially in the low-to-mid range.
  • FPS: Frames per second – how often the display and spectrum graph update. Values around 20–30 are normal; it doesn’t affect the audio, just how smooth the visual feels.
  • Voice-aware ducking: The code constantly listens to the mic’s mid-band energy (roughly 200–3000 Hz, where your voice lives). When it detects you talking, it smoothly reduces the masker level (up to about −12 dB) so passengers and phone calls can hear you more clearly.
  • Logging: Every ~0.25 s the code can record a row with timestamp, RMS dB, noise type, level, ducking gain, and 16 coarse spectrum bands. The Download CSV button bundles those rows into a file you can open in Excel, Google Sheets, or send to someone for analysis (for example, comparing different speeds, tire types, or settings).
  • Under the hood (high-level view):
    • The page uses the browser’s Web Audio API to create a small audio graph.
    • A custom “noise generator” node produces Pink/Brown/Gray noise samples in real time.
    • That noise passes through a gain node (controlled by the Level slider and ducking) and then out to whatever audio route the phone is using (ideally Bluetooth to the car).
    • If you tap Start listening, the microphone stream is sent into Analyser nodes. One analyser feeds the RMS meter and ducking logic; another feeds the spectrum visualiser and the logged spectrum values.
    • An animation loop redraws the spectrum and updates FPS/RMS on screen. A timer periodically samples the latest values and saves them to the log buffer for CSV download.
